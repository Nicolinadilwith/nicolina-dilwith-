# [Working title] Research Brief

## The challenge

When COVID first hit, everyone had to stay home, and no one was bringing their dogs in for
daycare since they could stay home with them all day. I thought I would need to dissolve the
program since we were only serving "essential" workers such as nurses and firefighters.
However, being the bleeding heart (and PR-sensitive) person that I am, I preemptively offered
special deals and discounts to our "heroes" since they were on the front lines of this disease
and getting quite overworked. Our clients liked that, and it was the only reason we were able
to continue, but it almost wasn't worth it for us. Luckily, we didn't have to shut down
completely during COVID because "animal services" were considered essential.

During the time that most people were working from home or quarantining, they got dogs to keep
them company. When restrictions lifted, many of these dogs' families returned to work and their
dogs had no idea how to be alone — lots of co-dependent relationships and separation anxiety on
both ends. That led to a boom of dogs needing care, and we blew up (in a good way). Lots of
people treat their dogs very well and will spend a lot of money on their care. However, Hawaii
is still behind compared to the rest of the United States when it comes to the "bougie"
treatment that some dogs get. It was good for us in the long run, and the question is whether
this boom will be sustainable and how we should price ourselves to remain competitive as a
"high end" daycare with field trips and highly trained staff.

Hawaii's gap isn't just different taste in dogs — a lot of the state is still rural, with dogs
kept as outside dogs or guard dogs. On the mainland, malls, strip malls, cafes, and restaurants
in walkable cities or places with a lot of young people are often dog-friendly or have water
bowls outside. Hawaii doesn't have any place like this. The closest is Kaka'ako, and even that
isn't at the level of dog-friendliness of many mainland places.

## Hypothesis

I'm not sure the boom is permanent — my own growth has plateaued, not kept increasing the way
it once did. Dogs don't live that long, so the COVID puppies that couldn't handle being alone
will keep aging, but once a family gets a new dog — either as a sibling or to replace the
original one — that dog grows up in a "regular" world, conditioned to being alone sometimes,
and not every single owner will be so desperate for care. So I think the boom is a temporary,
cohort-driven demand shock (tied to that specific generation of lockdown-raised dogs), not a
permanent shift in how people treat their dogs — it should taper as that generation is replaced.

Separately, and on a longer timeline, I think Hawaii is behind the mainland on
pricing/positioning because Hawaii lacks the dog-friendly infrastructure (walkable,
dog-friendly retail and cafe districts) that mainland cities have built up — that's a gap that
won't close just because the COVID boom happens to be fading.
