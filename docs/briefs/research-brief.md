# [Working title] Research Brief

## The challenge

When COVID-19 first hit, people stayed home with their dogs full-time, and demand for daycare
disappeared almost overnight. I expected to have to shut the program down, since we were only
serving "essential" workers such as nurses and firefighters. Wanting to support them, I
preemptively offered discounts to these workers, who were on the front lines of the pandemic and
increasingly overworked. That decision was the main reason we survived, though it came close to
not being worth it financially. We were fortunate that "animal services" were classified as
essential, so we never had to close entirely.

While most people were working from home or quarantining, many acquired dogs for companionship.
When restrictions lifted and those owners returned to work, their dogs had never learned to be
alone, leading to widespread separation anxiety and dependency on both sides. That produced a
surge in demand for daycare, and our business grew substantially as a result. Dog owners in
general are often willing to spend heavily on their pets' care, but Hawaii still lags behind the
rest of the United States in the kind of premium ("bougie") pet care that has become common on
the mainland. The boom benefited us in the long run, but it raises two open questions: whether
that demand is sustainable, and how we should price ourselves to remain competitive as a
high-end daycare offering field trips and highly trained staff.

Hawaii's gap isn't simply a difference in attitudes toward dogs. Much of the state remains
rural, where dogs are more often kept outdoors or as guard animals. On the mainland, malls,
cafes, and restaurants in walkable cities — especially those with younger populations — are
frequently dog-friendly, often providing water bowls or allowing dogs inside. Hawaii has no
comparable environment; the closest example, Kaka'ako, still falls short of that level of
dog-friendliness.

## Hypothesis

I don't believe the demand boom is permanent. My own business's growth has plateaued rather than
continuing to climb the way it once did. Because dogs have a limited lifespan, the "COVID
puppies" that never learned to be alone will continue to age, and as families acquire new dogs —
either as companions for the original dog or as replacements for it — those dogs will grow up in
a more typical environment, conditioned to periods of solitude. Not every future dog owner will
be as reliant on daycare as the current generation of owners is. I therefore expect the boom to
be a temporary, cohort-driven demand shock tied to this specific generation of lockdown-raised
dogs, rather than a lasting shift in how people care for their dogs — and I expect it to taper
off as that generation is gradually replaced.

Separately, and on a longer time horizon, I believe Hawaii lags behind the mainland in pricing
and positioning because it lacks the dog-friendly infrastructure — walkable retail and cafe
districts that welcome dogs — that many mainland cities have developed. That gap is independent
of the pandemic-driven boom and will not close simply because the boom itself is fading.
